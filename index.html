My planet: Mars

If I could pick any planet to live on, I pick Mars. It is rocky, has ice under the ground, and a day almost the same length as ours. It is named after the Roman god of war because of its blood-red colour, which comes from iron oxide: rust in the soil.

Why I chose it

Because it is the one place humans might really live one day, not just visit. Everything in this logbook uses real data. Only the person living it is imaginary.

— 1 —

Arriving and settling in

Sol 1Arrival

Seven months of falling, then dust

The trip took about seven months. Launches only work when Earth and Mars line up, roughly every 26 months. I stepped out into a pink-butterscotch sky and heard my own pulse in my helmet.

Fact: Mars has only about 11% of Earth’s mass, yet its land area is close to all of Earth’s dry land, because Mars has no oceans.
Sol 3My home

I live underground on purpose

Mars has no global magnetic field and a thin atmosphere, so radiation reaches the ground easily. Our habitat sits inside a lava tube, a tunnel left by ancient volcanoes, with rock overhead as a free shield. Our “windows” are screens linked to outside cameras.

Sol 12Air and water

Making oxygen from the sky

NASA’s Perseverance rover carried MOXIE, an experiment that split Martian carbon dioxide into oxygen. Our colony uses a much bigger version. Water comes from ice just under the soil, which we filter because Martian soil contains toxic perchlorate salts.

Fact: Liquid water cannot last on the surface. The air pressure is so low that it boils or freezes away.

Sol 25Food

Dinner is part science project

Most meals are dried or packaged. Fresh greens grow in a sealed greenhouse with Martian-soil substitutes and artificial light, because real Martian soil needs washing to remove toxic salts.

Fact: Astronauts on the International Space Station have already eaten lettuce grown in space, and researchers on Earth have grown crops in simulated Martian soil.

Sky, storms and moons

Sol 90The sky

The sunsets are blue

By day the sky is butterscotch because fine red dust hangs in the air. At sunset the dust scatters blue light forward, so the glow around the Sun turns icy blue. Then Phobos rises in the west, crossing the sky about three times a day.

Fact: Phobos is only about 22 km wide and is slowly drifting closer to Mars. It may break apart in tens of millions of years.
Sol 200Storm

The planet goes dim

A regional dust storm grew until it wrapped the whole planet, something that only happens on Mars. The wind is weak in the thin air, but the dust is fine, clings to everything and blocks sunlight from our solar panels. We rationed power and played cards by lamplight.

Fact: A global dust storm in 2018 cut contact with NASA’s solar-powered rover Opportunity, which had worked for almost 15 years.
Sol 260Exploring

My backyard is a record breaker

On weekends we drive out to see the sights: Olympus Mons, a volcano about 22 km high, and Valles Marineris, a canyon about 4,000 km long and up to 7 km deep. The Grand Canyon would fit inside it many times over.

Every “hello” takes minutes

Would I stay?

Sol 669Year one ends

My first Mars year

A Mars year lasts 687 Earth days, so the seasons are nearly twice as long as ours. I expected to miss home, and I did. But I also learned to love a blue sunset, a day just 39 minutes longer than yours, and a horizon that no human has ever walked to the end of.

What I love

  • Blue sunsets and two moons
  • Jumping three times higher
  • Ice for water right under my feet
  • Days that feel like home

What is hard

  • Thin air, so a suit outside
  • Radiation, so I live underground
  • Cold of −63 °C on average
  • Messages that take minutes
My verdict: Mars is a planet of extremes but not an impossible one. It would be a hard life, and a life full of wonder.
